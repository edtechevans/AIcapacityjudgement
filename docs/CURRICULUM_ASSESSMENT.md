# Curriculum and Assessment Guide — Grades 6–10

## Purpose

The Learning Studio develops **two related but separate competencies**. Students become increasingly able to understand, use and create with AI, while also becoming better at deciding *whether, when, why and under what conditions* to use it.

**Completion of the website's lessons is not evidence of mastery.** A strong assessment uses several kinds of evidence, including a student's reasoning, real work, tested revision, and transfer to a different subject or situation.

## Research-informed investigation upgrade (v20)

Each of the 30 Grade 6–10 modules now includes an original, discipline-transferable **inquiry question, investigation method, evidence expectation and new-context challenge**. These appear at the beginning of the Apply stage, and the evidence expectation is repeated in reflection and included in downloadable work notes. Teachers should provide appropriate sources or off-site materials for genuine investigations; prompts alone do not establish that an investigation has been performed.

Five modules include a no-account, fictional interactive laboratory integrated into guided practice. Students must explore two contrasting conditions before attempting the associated knowledge application. The resulting figures describe **invented teaching cases**. They are not estimates of the performance of real systems or of learners' ability.

Four modules have a more substantial, authentic design or governance dossier. An acceptable product should include problem definition, stakeholder considerations, reasoned AI-use or non-use, an artefact or policy prototype, evaluation evidence, revision and honest limitations. These projects may take multiple lessons; the website's estimated module duration does not substitute for scheduling real creation and feedback time.

See [Curriculum Rebuild v20](CURRICULUM_REBUILD_V20.md) and [Teacher Labs Guide](TEACHER_LABS_GUIDE.md) for lab mechanics and safeguarding guidance.

### Moderation questions before concluding that learning has occurred

- **Mechanism:** Can the learner explain *why* the result changed when one condition changed?
- **Data and uncertainty:** Did they report test scope and a limitation instead of overgeneralising a percentage?
- **Human purpose:** Can they defend a legitimate AI-use, limited-use or non-use choice against alternatives?
- **Authenticity:** Does their work respond to an identifiable user need or meaningful subject question?
- **Iteration:** Is there an observable revision linked to a test result or stakeholder comment?
- **Transfer:** Can they apply the idea to an unfamiliar example without being shown the original answer?

Learner writing is deliberately **not auto-scored**. For students who cannot use a live AI service or who prefer to speak, draw or work offline, an equivalent evidence-based response should remain possible.

## What the student actually learns in every module

The learning sequence is deliberately **explanation-first**:
1. **Learn:** Three concept explanations with an optional device read-aloud, vocabulary and a short formative retrieval check.
2. **Model:** Compare an inadequate approach with a better one and discuss what changed and why.
3. **Practise:** Complete a guided interactive activity, including explaining choices for decisions, evidence evaluation and prompt comparison.
4. **Apply:** Choose a relevant audience and challenge, select an appropriate mode of expression, make or test something authentic, and explain feedback and revision.
5. **Reflect:** Review the evidence of understanding, practice and application, then identify a transfer opportunity.

Independent learning does not mean unsupported learning. Allow pairs, teacher-provided source packs, accessible formats, language supports and direct adult guidance where appropriate. Encourage students to compare ideas, question assumptions and retain ownership.

## Assessment continuum

| Dimension | Beginning | Developing | Applying | Transferring |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **AI Capacity** | Recognises common AI tasks and works through guided examples | Uses approved AI or curated outputs to try a familiar approach with support | Selects, tests, evaluates and improves an appropriate approach for a genuine task | Compares alternatives and adapts a well-evidenced process to an unfamiliar, complex challenge |
| **AI Judgement** | Identifies a basic reliability, learning or privacy concern | Explains likely benefits and risks in familiar contexts | Defends a proportionate AI-use or non-use decision with evidence and safeguards | Balances competing priorities, uncertainty, affected people and consequences in unfamiliar situations |
| **Learning ownership** | Can name their own role with support | Makes some independent choices and responds to feedback | Explains why they accepted, adapted or rejected suggestions | Independently frames a problem, seeks perspectives, revises and justifies their contribution |
| **Transfer** | Identifies a familiar example | Suggests a possible connection to another task | Uses the strategy successfully in a different subject or real context | Generalises, critiques and refines an approach in an unfamiliar context |

The descriptors should be interpreted relative to the age and experience of a learner; do not equate all Grade 6 work with the first column or all Grade 10 work with the last.

## Evidence educators should look for

- **Conceptual understanding:** Can the learner explain what the system does and a relevant limitation?
- **Purposeful use:** Did the learner choose a tool and workflow for a reason connected to the task?
- **Judgement:** Can they explain a meaningful decision to use, limit or avoid AI?
- **Verification:** Did they inspect the quality of evidence and represent uncertainty appropriately?
- **Authorship:** Can they point to original choices and explain what they created or changed?
- **Audience and impact:** Did they consider people affected by the output and test it with them when possible?
- **Feedback and revision:** Can they show a before/after change and explain what prompted it?
- **Transfer:** Can they apply a strategy to a novel question without simply repeating the same quiz answer?

Use **samples, conferences, observations and authentic products**, not platform completion counts, as sources of assessment evidence. For teacher feedback, consider one concise strength and one specific next move in Capacity and Judgement respectively.

## Subject integration examples

- **G6 Science:** Explain an AI-supported classification claim, check it against a scientific reference, then explain it to younger learners.
- **G7 Individuals & Societies:** Compare AI-generated historical claims with credible records and perspectives; prepare an accurate exhibition label.
- **G8 Design:** Listen to a genuine user need, prototype an inclusive solution, test and revise it with an identified audience.
- **G9 Language & Literature:** Analyse synthetic media, create authentic messaging and evaluate its credibility, representation and consequences.
- **G10 Personal/Interdisciplinary projects:** Compare AI, non-AI and blended approaches, implement a justified choice, gather feedback and defend the consequences of that choice.

Where school policies restrict a given AI tool or age group, use teacher-curated examples or offline simulations. No module should force students to submit private information to public tools.

## Evaluating an intervention

A small class pilot should check whether students **learn something they can explain**, apply something they did not do before, and show more informed decisions in a fresh context. The starting six-question snapshot is a formative signal only; it cannot reliably classify a student's capability or judgement.

For a ten-student first study, see [Student Pilot Guide](STUDENT_PILOT_GUIDE.md).

## Privacy and deployment limits

Public GitHub Pages does not provide validated school authentication, teacher assessment storage or secure portfolio submissions. Free-text project notes remain in browser memory and are not uploaded; only module progress and check-in choices are stored in local browser storage. Work samples need to be saved in **approved school systems** if teachers wish to evaluate them. Do not collect identifiable records through this preview.
