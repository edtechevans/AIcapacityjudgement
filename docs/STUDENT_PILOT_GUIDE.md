# Student Learning Pilot — Ten Learners (Grades 6–10)

**Purpose:** Test whether the Learning Studio helps students *learn and apply* AI capability and AI judgement, rather than simply recognise the correct response.

**Status:** Protocol ready for a school-run pilot. No student feedback has yet been collected.

## Participants and safeguarding

Invite **two students from each grade (6, 7, 8, 9, 10)**, choosing varied levels of confidence, prior tool experience, language background, learning needs and interests. Obtain the school's required permission and follow its safeguarding procedures. Use **school-approved tools only**; do not enter personal student information into the public preview. Assign anonymous IDs (e.g. P01–P10) only in a school-approved record.

## Student session (approximately 45 minutes)

1. **2 minutes — Context and confidence.** Ask: "When have you used AI to help with learning? When would you avoid it?" Do not grade confidence.
2. **4 minutes — Independent entry.** Students select their grade, navigate their library and choose a module. Observe where they hesitate or request help.
3. **8–10 minutes — Learn.** Students work through the three explanations and vocabulary. Ask them to explain one core idea without looking at the screen. Record what they can accurately describe.
4. **5 minutes — Model and practise.** Students compare the approaches, complete the retrieval item and guided challenge, and interpret feedback. Note whether incorrect feedback actually improves their thinking.
5. **15 minutes — Apply and test.** Students choose a real or classroom-simulated challenge, decide whether AI belongs, create a small work sample (or sketch/presentation), test it or seek feedback, and describe a revision.
6. **5 minutes — Transfer task.** Pose a new scenario in another subject. Ask what they would do, whether AI would help, which risks or limits matter, and how they would verify the outcome.
7. **5 minutes — Interview.** Ask questions below; do not coach towards a preferred response.

## Interview prompts

- What did you understand now that you did not understand before?
- Where did you have to think hardest? Which part felt too obvious?
- Did you make a real choice or did the website mostly tell you what to do?
- Show one way you could use what you learned in a current subject or problem.
- What would help you learn more independently? More examples, audio, pictures, vocabulary, feedback or time?
- Did a mistake help you learn? What did the feedback change?
- Would you voluntarily choose another lesson, and why?
- Who might benefit from the product you created? How would you check that with them?

## What counts as success

**Do not evaluate success by quiz scores or enjoyment alone.** Seek evidence that students can:
- Explain a concept accurately without simply reading it back.
- Make and justify a purposeful AI-use *or non-use* decision.
- Create or test something beyond selecting a correct answer.
- Notice an error and improve their work after feedback.
- Transfer their learning to a new situation.

Analyse differences by grade and starting experience, without inferring fixed capability types from six snapshot questions. Compare student work with teacher judgement, not just site completion marks.

## Privacy and technical boundaries

The site is a **public static preview**. Progress is local to the device and text drafts are ephemeral; there is no school authentication or teacher dashboard. Do **not** treat site completion as a validated student mastery score. Do not collect identifiable student feedback or sensitive data via the public GitHub site. Keep actual pilot records in approved school systems.

## After ten sessions

Review recurring problems first: inaccessible instructions, weak explanations, confusing prompts, overly obvious options, missing work samples, and lack of meaningful transfer. Rework three modules, retest them with students, then expand changes across the grade progression.
