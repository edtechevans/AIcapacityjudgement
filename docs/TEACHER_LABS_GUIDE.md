# Facilitator Guide: Ten Interactive AI Investigation Labs

**Audience:** G6–12 educators and curriculum reviewers. These original simulations are fictional and work without AI-service accounts, network requests or student submissions. The live website stores existing practice checkpoints locally; laboratory choices are session-only.

## Structure for any lab (25–40 minutes)

**5 minutes — Predict.** Introduce the driving question without showing results. Ask students what they expect to change and what will stay constant. Use a sketch, oral response or quick pair discussion.

**10 minutes — Investigate.** Students run one laboratory setting, write a prediction or observation, then change the condition and compare. Ask *what changed and what did not*, not merely which result was higher.

**10 minutes — Explain.** Students interpret the result against its deliberately simplified assumptions. Have them state a conclusion with a boundary: *“On these examples, we found…, but we cannot conclude…”*.

**10–15 minutes — Transfer.** Students apply the reasoning in a second subject or authentic school scenario, seek feedback and revise an evidence-backed claim. Record only work approved for the school's assessment environment.

## G6 A1 — AI, fixed rules and uncertainty

**Question:** Does a clever output automatically mean a system uses AI?

Students compare a fictional digital timer with a toy text labeler and an unfamiliar app. They change an input and observe what happens. Explain that these are illustrative rules and fabricated outputs, not a claim that the text labeler contains a truly trained machine-learning model. For the unfamiliar app, **the correct conclusion may be that there is not enough evidence**.

**Facilitate:** Use three illustrated process cards and ask what input, process and output each has. Invite students to predict the change before pressing a control. Highlight that both fixed rules and trained models can respond differently to new inputs; students must investigate how a system is made.

**Transfer challenge:** Investigate a library kiosk or school recommendation tool and determine what evidence would be needed before naming its underlying process.

**Offline alternative:** Three printed process cards and two teacher-authored inputs for each. No live AI tool required.

## G7 A1 — Training examples and shortcuts

**Question:** How do training examples change results when an algorithm attends to a misleading feature?

The toy model finds the nearest labelled training example using **shape difference + 2 × lighting difference**. The limited set contains an oak in bright light and a maple in dim light. Learners can now independently add or remove the missing oak-in-shade and maple-in-bright-light examples, and adjust the lighting feature's influence from zero to three times leaf shape. The more varied preset includes both kinds in both conditions. The same four new fictional photos are evaluated in both conditions. The toy score changes from **2/4 correct to 4/4 correct**.

**Facilitate:** Have students identify which of the four examples changed, then demonstrate the distance rule with a short table or manipulatives. Explain the difference between the simple, teacher-made classifier and a real trained image-recognition model. Do not imply that more data always improves fairness.

**Transfer challenge:** What groups or conditions might be missing from real datasets? What would count as a representative test?

**Offline alternative:** Give learners two training cards, four test cards and a distance-rule strip; then add two training cards and recompute.

## G8 B2 — Optimisation and missing voices

**Question:** Who is seen when a feed optimises for engagement versus credible, varied perspectives?

The lab has five invented school-community posts. One ranking uses a fabricated attention metric; the other uses **65% credibility and 35% representation**. This is a simple transparent scoring model, not a replica of any platform. Learners can change the evidence-versus-representation weighting within the evidence-oriented feed. The scores intentionally encode designers' priorities; discuss the limitations and subjectivity of even the “better” metric.

**Facilitate:** Ask who decides the metrics, who might be misrepresented and whether any ranking rule should be used without consultation. Distinguish the reliability of a claim from the amount of attention it receives.

**Transfer challenge:** In a research results page, identify the missing voices and what sources would help evaluate coverage.

**Offline alternative:** Print five fictional post cards and have groups rank them under two stated rules.

## G9 A1 — Reliability and denominator honesty

**Question:** Can a model tested on familiar examples be called reliable?

The fictional service passes **4/4 familiar cases**, but passes **5/8 combined cases** after adding four harder examples. Outcomes are invented to illustrate test design, not performance of an actual service. Learners can also give consequential failures extra concern points; this is a discussion model rather than a valid real-world harm score.

**Facilitate:** Avoid interpreting 5/8 as a real-world error rate. Ask learners to define the user population, failure severity, cases excluded, what independent evaluations are needed and why some errors carry greater consequences.

**Transfer challenge:** Create a model evaluation card identifying scope, methods, metrics, limitations, failures and a reasoned recommendation.

**Offline alternative:** Provide two versions of the same fictional results table with different samples.

## G9 B1 — Chatbot trust, personal boundaries and agency

**Question:** How should someone respond when an AI system implies secrecy or replaces trusted relationships?

**Safety guidance:** Present the chatbot as a **fictional third-person scenario**. Do not ask students to disclose real mental health experiences, personal messages, family information or private AI use. Provide a neutral alternative role-play for students who prefer not to discuss chatbot relationships. The scenario is about boundaries and system behaviour, not a diagnosis or a judgement of a student's relationships.

**Facilitate:** Explain that a tool can produce empathetic language without providing human accountability. Students should understand not to enter private information into an unapproved tool and that seeking a trusted adult or appropriate human support is an available choice.

**Transfer challenge:** Write a brief age-appropriate guide on appropriate use, privacy, independent thinking and help-seeking. Allow students to use a fictional example rather than their personal life.

## G10 A3 — Capstone design decisions

**Question:** What makes a solution actually useful and accountable to people who need it?

The fictional challenge is a multilingual, accessible school welcome resource. Learners can prioritise inclusive access, information privacy or speed and explain how that changes the proposed safeguards. Students compare **automation-first, human-only and carefully bounded blended** design options. There is no automatically correct choice: all have advantages, risks and possible safeguards.

**Facilitate:** Require a problem definition, stakeholders, comparison criteria, prototype, test plan including an edge case, feedback from an approved audience or teacher-provided persona, revised artefact and a defensible decision record.

**Transfer challenge:** Have students defend how they would change the process if their first prototype failed for a different language group or an accessibility need.

## Classroom pacing

Individual introduction and laboratory sessions may fit a standard lesson, but authentic creation and revision often require longer. The in-site Facilitator resources now provide realistic grade-level estimates, preparation guidance and offline alternatives. Grade 10's design capstone is planned over approximately four 70-minute sessions plus a feedback conference. Do not pressure students to complete actual prototypes during a short demonstration.

## Inclusion, assessment and data boundaries

Offer read-aloud, subtitles/transcripts for any teacher-added videos, bilingual vocabulary and alternative expression through speech, sketches, tables or writing. Maintain a keyboard-operable pathway. Neither the fictional settings nor the answers should be mistaken for medical, social, psychological or academic evaluations.

A real assessment requires the *reasoning* behind the choice, appropriate citations, an authentic work sample and transfer beyond the practice item. Teachers should document one strength and one specific next step, rather than infer mastery from lesson completion.

For any deployment with authenticated students, use approved SharePoint/Entra architecture and server-authorised grade access, not these public preview controls.
## DP1 and DP2 advanced inquiry laboratories

**G11 A1 · System evaluation:** Compare familiar, shifted and access-related synthetic cases. Change threshold and the relative concern assigned to false exclusions. Compute and explain the four-case results, but never present them as genuine admissions performance or a certified fairness score. Ask learners to propose an independent test and defensible stop rule.

**G11 B2 · Evidence provenance:** Map five articles and their original sources. Students compare headline counts with verifiable independent origins, then critique the quality of methods and uncertainty. Paper alternative: source cards linked with arrows and a separate methods audit.

**G12 A2 · Workflow stress test:** Compare autonomous, bounded verified and human-led publication processes under three injected failures—missing source, outage and private data. Students design an accountable verification and recovery route. Paper alternative: process maps and tool-permission/incident cards.

**G12 B3 · Governance and redress:** Examine a fictional consequential error and the response to an affected user with varying language/access needs. Students propose an accessible appeal, independent review, correction and conditions for restart. Concealment is intentionally a poor fictional option for critique, not policy advice. Paper alternative: an ethically framed stakeholder hearing.

**DP projects:** G11 A3 is an independent stakeholder design dossier, normally three substantial studios plus feedback. G12 A3 is an authentic impact portfolio, approximately six to eight weekly blocks and an audience conference. Neither can be completed responsibly merely by ticking the website's practice checklist. Seek original work, consent and approvals, test results, revisions and a human defence. Connections to TOK, Extended Essay and CAS are exploratory only: follow the school's current IB and subject academic-integrity rules.
